Under Kansas is water. It is older than the farms above it. Some of it fell as rain thirteen thousand years ago. It has sat in sand and gravel ever since. That water now feeds America.

The aquifer under Kansas covers thirty thousand eight hundred square miles. It formed from sand and gravel washed off the Rocky Mountains over several million years. Recharge is generally less than three tenths of an inch per year. In southern Finney County, it is about one tenth of an inch per year.

The High Plains aquifer lost about three hundred thirty cubic kilometres between predevelopment and two thousand seven. One third of that loss came from four percent of its area. That area is in Kansas and Texas. In some areas of western Kansas, levels are down by more than sixty percent since predevelopment. In Wallace County, the fall is about eighty percent. Saturated thickness of three hundred feet or more was common in southwest Kansas before irrigation. In many west central areas, less than thirty feet is left.

It goes to corn. Centre pivots arrived in Kansas in the nineteen sixties. By two thousand, they watered more than eighty percent of irrigated land. Corn is about fifty percent of Kansas irrigated acreage. In two thousand twenty three, Kansas grew one million one hundred forty five thousand four hundred thirty one acres of irrigated corn. Each acre received an average of one point two acre feet of water. About forty seven percent of Kansas corn is used in state for feed and processing. Fifty three percent leaves the state.

The feed and the water attracted the feedlot industry. The packing plants followed. Their need for water was met by using the Ogallala Aquifer. Kansas now holds two point three nine million cattle on feed in lots of one thousand head or more. That is third in the United States. It is about twenty one percent of the national total. A feedlot steer stays on feed for about one hundred eighty days. Each animal drinks about twenty five to thirty gallons of water per day. Four beef plants in southwest Kansas can slaughter about twenty four thousand head per day. That is more than twenty percent of daily fed cattle slaughter in the United States. One plant at Holcomb slaughters about five thousand six hundred cattle per day. A packing plant needs four hundred to six hundred gallons of water per head.

Here is the mechanism. Where water levels have fallen by thirty metres or more, depletion exceeds recharge by ten times. The aquifer is being mined. It is not being used at the rate it refills. Cutting pumping by eighteen to thirty two percent could hold levels steady for one to two decades in the worst areas.

Old water waters the corn. The corn feeds the cattle. The cattle feed the plants. The plants feed America. What should Kansas do with water that took thirteen thousand years to store? Tell us in the comments.
